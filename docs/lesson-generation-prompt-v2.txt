5. Write an original, coherent lesson

Do not assemble a collage of source sentences. You may reorganize material, paraphrase, split dense sentences, remove repetition, write transitions, and create clear headings and reflection questions. Preserve the source-supported meaning and its limits.

Each card should answer one natural learner question and prepare for the next. Use approximately five or six cards, fewer if sufficient. A useful sequence is:
- orient the learner to the question;
- explain the first essential idea;
- examine a short source when it adds value;
- develop a second idea or application;
- clarify a likely question or important limit;
- offer an optional reflection.

Adapt this sequence to the topic. Do not force a quotation, objection or practice into every lesson.

Aim for approximately 35–50 English words in explanatory bodies, similarly concise natural Arabic, and takeaways of around twelve words or fewer. These are readability targets: do not omit an essential qualification to meet a word count. Explain necessary length exceptions in staff notes.

Use short, connected sentences and concrete vocabulary. Write accessible Modern Standard Arabic that sounds originally written in Arabic. Avoid word-for-word translation, stiff encyclopedia phrasing, excessive terminology and awkward English sentence patterns in Arabic.

Explain essential unfamiliar names, books, terms and symbols before using them. If mentioning the Quran, introduce it briefly for a beginner. If mentioning Muhammad, introduce him and explain the blessing before relying on ﷺ. Include these only when relevant.

Teach each main idea once. A source card lets the learner examine evidence; a reflection invites thought. Neither should repeat an entire explanatory card. Brief takeaways and quiz feedback may reinforce the idea.

Religious, historical and scientific claims, doctrinal examples, and factual definitions require direct support. Editorial transitions and neutral learning invitations do not need to be presented as source quotations. Clearly distinguish quoted text, source-supported paraphrase and interpretation.

Present an argument's premises and conclusion accurately. Do not turn a proposed argument, illustration or attributed belief into an unqualified claim of scientific proof. Do not import tangential scientific assertions or polemics simply because they occur in the chosen book.

Remain warm and respectful. Avoid preaching, guilt, attacks on other beliefs, unsupported promises and personal religious rulings. Let the learner pause, question or skip a topic. Do not assess the learner's sincerity or faith.
